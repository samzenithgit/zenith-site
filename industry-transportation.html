Transportation & infrastructure economic impact studies

Our approach

Highway construction, rail expansion, transit systems and bridge replacements support economic activity through construction spending, ongoing operations and better connections between places. An economic impact study quantifies these contributions, providing the data agencies need for grant applications, legislative testimony, ballot measures, and public communication.

We work with state departments of transportation, transit agencies, metropolitan planning organizations, and private operators on studies ranging from single-project construction impacts to system-wide operational analyses.

Our method for transportation studies

We report construction, operations and user benefits as separate results. Construction is a one-time shock: the capital budget split into design and engineering, earthwork, paving, structures, systems and materials. Right-of-way purchases are left out because buying existing land does not create new production. Operations and maintenance recur each year: agency payroll, contracted services, fuel, parts and vehicle maintenance for a transit system, or routine maintenance for a road or bridge.

Travel time savings, lower vehicle operating costs and fewer crashes are user benefits, and they belong in a benefit-cost analysis. USDOT's BCA guidance treats economic impact analysis as a different exercise whose results are not additive benefits. An impact study treats project spending as a stimulus rather than a cost and does not net out losses in other regions, so the two answer different questions. For grant applications we keep them apart, and any BCA figures follow the current USDOT guidance and parameter values.

Funding source also matters. Federal and state money from outside the study region is new to it. Local tax dollars would have been spent locally on something else, and we note that when the audience is local.

Typical inputs we ask for

  • Capital cost estimate by work type, and by year
  • Funding sources by level of government
  • Construction schedule
  • Annual operating and maintenance budget, with agency payroll
  • Ridership or traffic forecasts, if user benefits are in scope
  • Any benefit-cost analysis already prepared for the project

Questions clients ask

Can your economic impact figures go into our federal grant BCA?

No. USDOT's guidance says economic impact results are not additive benefits in a BCA, and its grant-specific BCA instructions repeat that point. We report jobs, income and output in their own section of the application narrative or in a standalone report, and keep them out of the benefit-cost ratio.

Are construction jobs permanent jobs?

No. IMPLAN counts a job as one job held for one year, full-time or part-time, so construction results are job-years spread across the build. A three-year build therefore appears in the total as three years of job counts added together. We show jobs by year and label them as temporary so readers do not mistake them for permanent positions.

Do you measure connectivity and safety benefits?

Where the data allow, we describe and quantify them, using the USDOT recommended values for travel time and crash costs. We report those results apart from jobs and output, because adding monetized user benefits to an economic impact total counts the same effect twice.
