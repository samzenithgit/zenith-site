Airport economic impact studies

Our approach

Airports support jobs at airlines, concessions, cargo operators, maintenance shops and ground transportation companies. Arriving visitors spend money at hotels, restaurants and shops across the region. An economic impact study puts figures on both, for capital planning, grant applications and conversations with elected officials and the public.

We produce studies that follow the FAA framework, for single general aviation fields and for statewide systems such as Montana's 75 airports. Our work includes on-airport employment surveys, passenger and visitor intercept surveys, cargo analysis, and capital investment impact modeling.

Our method for airport studies

Our airport studies use the structure set out in the FAA report Estimating the Regional Economic Significance of Airports. Direct impacts are the activity of employers on the airfield: airlines, the airport sponsor, FBOs, concessions, cargo operators, maintenance shops and federal agencies. We measure them with an on-airport tenant survey of employment, payroll and local purchases, checked against airport records.

The FAA report labels off-airport visitor spending at hotels, restaurants, rental car counters and shops as "indirect." In IMPLAN, indirect means supply-chain purchases, so we use the FAA labels in airport reports and explain the difference in the methodology section. Visitor spending comes from passenger and pilot surveys that record origin, trip purpose, nights stayed and spending. Residents returning home are not visitors and are excluded.

Direct and visitor impacts are the first-order effects. We then apply regional multipliers for the later rounds of supplier and household spending and report those as a separate "induced" line, matching the FAA definition. Capital projects appear as one-time construction impacts. FAA benefit-cost guidance keeps these regional effects out of BCA measures, and our reports describe them as economic impact rather than project benefits.

Questions clients ask

Why does the report use "indirect" differently from other impact studies?

The FAA's 1992 guidance defines indirect impacts as off-airport activity attributable to the airport, mainly visitor spending, and induced impacts as all later rounds of spending. Most IMPLAN studies use indirect for supply-chain purchases. We follow the FAA terms in airport work so results line up with other state aviation system studies, and the methodology section maps each FAA category to the IMPLAN effects behind it.

Is a small general aviation airport worth studying?

Yes, and system studies show why. Our work for the Montana Department of Transportation covered all 75 airports in the state aviation system plan, including general aviation fields that support wildfire response, agricultural aviation and access to remote communities. Their totals are smaller than a commercial hub's, but documenting them airport by airport gives each sponsor figures to use in grant applications and with local officials.

Can these results go into the benefit-cost analysis for an AIP grant?

No. The FAA's 2026 Airport Benefit-Cost Analysis Guidance says regional employment and income gains should be listed separately from aviation user benefits and should not be included in BCA measures. It also cites OMB Circular A-94's exclusion of multiplier effects. An economic impact study supports legislative, community and funding conversations. A BCA measures user benefits such as time saved, using FAA parameters.
